# Dropshipping playbook — extracted, annotated

Source: a 2h47m course video supplied 2026-08-06. It is a funnel for a paid
mentorship, so the recommendations are a mix of real operating knowledge, monetised
referrals, and a few things that would actively damage HIVOLT. Everything below is
separated on that basis rather than reproduced flat.

---

## Part 1 — The parts worth keeping

### The five-step loop

```
product research → build store → marketing → fulfilment → scale
```

Sound, and it matches how the category actually operates.

### Winning-product traits — score a candidate out of 6, test at 4+

| Trait | Test |
|---|---|
| Easily advertisable | Passes Meta/TikTok/Google policy. Not edible, pharmaceutical, 18+, or counterfeit |
| Problem-solving | Fixes something specific the buyer already resents |
| Emotionally attaching | Someone buys it *for* another person |
| Healthy margin | Enough spread to absorb CAC. Cited as why Amazon FBA sellers quit — price races to the floor |
| Low availability, high demand | Not in Target, Walmart, or Amazon. Scarcity is doing work |
| Wow factor | Stops a scroll on its own |

The margin point is the one that matters most and is the one most often skipped.

### Platform routing

| Platform | Use when |
|---|---|
| Google | Buyers are already searching a specific named thing |
| Meta | Problem-solving, buyer roughly 25–50, older/practical niches |
| TikTok | Fun, novel, visually surprising, younger |

### Ad account structure

```
campaign   → the goal        (sales / conversion)
ad group   → the targeting   (who, where)
creative   → what they see   (the videos)
```

Practical settings named: schedule to start 00:00 next day rather than mid-session;
strip placements down to the one platform; disable ad comments on a brand with no
history; install and verify the pixel **before** spending anything; duplicate the
first ad group ×3 so four run in parallel for signal.

### Metrics and kill rules

| Metric | Threshold |
|---|---|
| CPC | under $1, hard ceiling $2 |
| CTR | above 1% |
| Cost per conversion | must sit under contribution margin |
| Test budget per product | $50–300 |
| Kill point | $150–200 with no traction |

### Scaling

- **Vertical** — raise budget on what works
- **Horizontal** — more audiences, more creatives, more campaigns

### UGC sourcing — the most immediately usable section

```
shortlist 20–50 creators  →  reach out  →  agree budget
   →  ship them the product  →  they film  →  you run it as an ad
```

$150–500 per video; bundles negotiable. Find them by searching "UGC creator" on
TikTok. Splitting one budget across three creators beats ten videos from one.

### Fulfilment structure

Supplier holds pre-purchased stock and ships per order — inventory is bought and
owned before it is sold, the supplier acting as shipping agent. Move to holding
your own stock at roughly $5–10k/month profit, at which point branding (packaging,
insert card, logo on the product) becomes the differentiator.

### COD vs prepaid

COD carries a high cancellation rate; prepaid drops it sharply. **Largely
inapplicable here** — COD is a GCC norm and effectively absent from US ecommerce.
The whole payment-gateway section of the video is UAE-specific.

## Part 2 — Three things in it that would undo this session's work

### 1. The image method is exactly what produced HIVOLT's 102 fabricated images

The video installs a browser extension to defeat right-click protection on a
competitor's site, copies their product photography, and feeds it to an image model
to regenerate. It then takes a photograph of a real person from Pinterest and
regenerates that person onto the product.

That is competitor imagery plus a real individual's likeness, neither licensed. The
presenter hedges — *"do your own due diligence"*, *"I'm not sure if I can sell it
or advertise it"* — which is the tell.

This session removed **102 fabricated images from 41 products** for precisely this
class of defect. Do not reintroduce it.

### 2. The testimonials are generated

At ~1:41:30 the review block is filled by asking a model for testimonials, which
returns "Best purchase this summer", "Looks even better in person", attributed to
"Jason S.", "Laya H.", "Michael T." A star rating of "4.9 / 4,000" is entered the
same way. He says out loud that real ones are better, then uses the invented ones.

In the US this is an FTC matter, not a style choice. HIVOLT has **zero orders**, so
every review on the site would be fabricated by definition. The storefront's whole
argument is "published, not claimed" — this would end it.

### 3. The worked example is arithmetically wrong, and wrong in the expensive direction

His figures: 1,195 AED price, ~500 AED landed cost, 10 units/day.

| | AED | USD |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (300 units) | 358,500 | $97,617 |
| Gross profit (300 × 695) | 208,500 | $56,773 |
| **He reported as profit** | **177,000** | — |

177,000 is revenue *minus* gross profit — that is COGS, reported as profit. But the
larger error is what follows: **he deducts no advertising at all**, calling it "not
going to be that crazy". Advertising is the dominant cost in this model.

| ROAS | Ad spend | Actual net |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0x | 179,250 | 29,250 AED ($7,965) |
| 2.5x | 143,400 | 65,100 AED ($17,726) |
| 3.0x | 119,500 | 89,000 AED ($24,234) |

Breakeven ROAS = 1.72x. The business can work — but at 2.5x ROAS the real figure is
**about a third of the number presented**, and the 10-units-a-day assumption
underneath it is unvalidated.

This is the same error the HIVOLT unit-economics workbook exists to prevent.

### Also worth naming

The Shopify signup, the Shrine theme and PPADS are all affiliate or referral links.
That doesn't make them bad tools. It does mean the recommendation isn't neutral.

## Part 3 — What this implies for HIVOLT

### HIVOLT scores 2/6 on his own checklist

| Trait | HIVOLT | |
|---|---|---|
| Easily advertisable | **Yes** | Activewear, no policy conflict |
| Problem-solving | Partial | Leggings going sheer is real; 220 g/m² addresses it |
| Emotionally attaching | No | Nobody buys a training tee for someone else |
| Healthy margin | **Unknown** | The Tapstitch cost gap — still the biggest open question |
| Low stock / high demand | **No** | Activewear is available in every shop on earth |
| Wow factor | No | It is a well-specified blank |

His threshold to test is 4+. HIVOLT sits at roughly 2. That is not a reason to stop
— it is an accurate description of why paid acquisition here is hard, and it agrees
with the Trendtrack finding that comparable US leggings stores sit near 6,200
monthly visits and are ~4 years old.

### The structural mismatch

**His entire method assumes a one-product store.** One product, one page, one
creative set, one $150–200 test, then kill or scale. HIVOLT is a 110-SKU general
activewear catalogue with no hero funnel. You cannot run his test on it — there is
nothing to point a single ad set at.

If paid acquisition is the route, the implication is concrete: **pick one product,
build a dedicated one-product funnel, and test that.** The rest of the catalogue
stays as the store; the funnel is what gets the traffic.

The Voltcore 2-Piece Set at $79 is the strongest candidate — it is the only SKU
with a named mechanism (220 g/m²), a bundle rationale, and a real spec sheet.

### What still comes first

None of this runs before the two open items:

1. A real card has never been processed by this store.
2. The seven Tapstitch cost lines are unfilled, so "healthy margin" — the trait his
   own framework weights heaviest — cannot be scored at all.

His own checklist cannot be completed for HIVOLT until item 2 is answered.
